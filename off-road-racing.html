How Race Teams Set Limit Straps Professional desert and rock-crawling teams don't guess at limit strap length — they dial them in systematically. The process starts with the vehicle on a four-post lift with all four wheels hanging freely at full droop.

A team member measures the distance between upper and lower mounting points, then checks three things: CV axle angle (must stay under 30 degrees), brake hose tension (must have slack remaining), and shock shaft position (must not be at full extension stop). The strap is set to the shortest of those three limiting factors. If the brake hose runs out of slack at 13 inches of droop but the CV joint is happy to 15 inches, the strap is set to 13. On high-speed desert builds, teams often set straps 10–15% shorter than the maximum safe droop to add a margin for the dynamic loads of high-speed hits.

For rock crawling, where maximum articulation is valuable, straps are typically set exactly at the CV limit to preserve every inch of wheel travel while keeping the drivetrain safe.

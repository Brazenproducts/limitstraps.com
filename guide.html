The Complete Guide to Limit Straps

Everything you need to know about protecting your suspension investment.

What Are Limit Straps?

Limit straps are heavy-duty webbing straps that connect your vehicle's frame (or body) to the axle or suspension arm. Their job is simple: prevent the suspension from extending beyond its safe range of travel. When your wheels drop into a hole, go over a jump, or the suspension fully unloads, limit straps catch the droop before something expensive breaks.

Think of them as a seatbelt for your suspension. They don't do anything during normal driving, but when things get extreme, they're the difference between driving home and calling a tow truck.

Why You Need Them

Without limit straps, full suspension droop can cause:

  • Shock shafts pulling out of the shock body, destroying the seal and the shock
  • CV joints over-extending and tearing boots or snapping axles
  • Brake lines stretching and eventually failing
  • Sway bar end links bending or breaking
  • Bump stop damage from violent rebound after full extension

A quality set of limit straps costs a fraction of a single shock rebuild. For lifted trucks, off-road rigs, and UTVs, they're not optional — they're essential maintenance.

How to Choose the Right Limit Straps

Length

This is the most critical measurement. The strap needs to be long enough to allow full normal suspension travel but short enough to stop extension before components reach their breaking point. Most manufacturers provide sizing guides based on your vehicle and lift height. When in doubt, measure the distance between your mounting points at full droop, then order straps 1-2" shorter than that measurement. Nylon webbing stretches ~1" per foot under load, so the straps will reach full extension naturally.

Material & Construction

Look for straps made from heavy-duty woven webbing — the same type of material used in tow straps and racing harnesses. Quad-wrapped construction (where the webbing is folded and stitched multiple times) provides the highest strength. Avoid cheap single-layer straps that can stretch or tear under load.

Hardware

Quality mounting hardware matters as much as the strap itself. Look for grade 8 bolts, reinforced mounting tabs, and proper bushings. The strap is only as strong as its weakest mounting point.

Application

Different vehicles need different straps:

  • Off-road race vehicles need the highest strength ratings and often require multiple straps per corner
  • Lifted trucks and SUVs typically need one strap per side, sized for their specific lift and shock travel
  • UTVs need shorter, lighter straps matched to their suspension geometry

Installation Basics

Most limit strap installations follow the same general process:

  • Identify mounting points on the frame and axle (or A-arm)
  • With the suspension at full droop, measure the distance between mounting points
  • Select the correct strap length based on your measurement
  • Bolt the strap to both mounting points using the provided hardware
  • Cycle the suspension through its full range to verify the strap engages before any component reaches its limit
  • Torque all hardware to spec and check after the first few rides

If you're not comfortable doing the installation yourself, any reputable off-road shop can install limit straps in under an hour. It's a straightforward job that doesn't require specialized tools.

Made in USA Matters

American-made straps use domestically sourced webbing with consistent tensile strength ratings, proper stitching patterns, and quality hardware. When a strap is the only thing between your suspension and a catastrophic failure, you want to know exactly what it's made of and who made it.

Complete Buying Guide — Guide

What Are Suspension Limit Straps?

Suspension limit straps — also called droop straps or travel limiters — prevent a vehicle's suspension from dropping below a safe limit when a wheel goes into full droop (hangs in the air). Without limit straps, full droop can cause CV axles to over-extend and tear, brake lines to stretch and crack, and ABS sensor wiring to snap. Limit straps are a cheap insurance policy against expensive drivetrain damage on lifted vehicles used off-road.

They are especially important after a suspension lift. A 4-inch lift adds 4 inches of downward travel range that the factory axles, lines, and wiring were not designed to handle. Properly set limit straps restrict that travel to a safe range — typically allowing 2–3 inches of additional droop over stock while protecting the drivetrain components.

How to Measure and Set Limit Straps

Measurement process: with the vehicle on a lift or jackstands with the wheels hanging, measure the distance from the upper mounting point to the lower mounting point at the maximum safe droop position. This is determined by checking CV axle angle (should not exceed 30–35 degrees), brake line tension, and ABS sensor wire slack. Set the strap length to this measurement — the strap should become taut just before the problematic droop point.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need limit straps with a suspension lift?

Yes, for any lift over 2 inches used off-road. Full droop on a lifted vehicle without limit straps can tear CV axles, snap brake lines, and damage ABS wiring. Limit straps are inexpensive insurance against expensive drivetrain repairs.

What is the difference between limit straps and bump stops?

Bump stops limit upward suspension travel (compression). Limit straps limit downward travel (droop/extension). Both are needed for a complete suspension protection setup. Many off-road builds run extended bump stops and limit straps together.

How long do limit straps last?

Quality nylon limit straps last 5–10 years under normal off-road use. Inspect them annually for fraying, UV degradation, and cracking at the mounting hardware. Replace immediately if you see fraying — a snapped limit strap at full droop can damage the components it was protecting.

Material Quality — What Matters

The single most important factor in a limit strap's performance is the webbing material. Here's how to evaluate:

Military-Spec Nylon (Recommended)

Mil-spec nylon webbing (MIL-W-4088 compliant) is the gold standard. It offers tensile strengths of 7,000–10,000+ lbs depending on width, excellent UV resistance (won't degrade in sunlight), and minimal stretch under load. The mil-spec limit straps at bullstrap.com use this material exclusively — Used by serious builders and race teams across the country.

Standard Nylon

Common in mid-tier kits. Good strength (4,000–6,000 lbs) but lower UV resistance than mil-spec. Fine for occasional off-road use, but won't last as long under constant sun exposure.

Polyester

Excellent UV resistance — polyester holds up well in prolonged sun exposure, making it a solid choice for vehicles that live outdoors. Good strength-to-cost ratio.

What to Avoid

Any strap that doesn't list a material specification is likely polypropylene or low-grade nylon — avoid these. No brand name and no specs = no accountability.

Mounting Hardware Options

Sewn loops (most common): A loop of webbing at each end. Bolt passes through the loop. Simple, reliable, and allows for angle adjustment.

Clevis ends: Fork-style metal ends that clip into a receiver. Easier to remove but adds complexity and potential failure points.

Bolt-through: Flat tab designed to bolt directly between frame and axle mount points. Cleanest look but requires precise spacing.

Should You Buy Custom or Off-the-Shelf?

✓ Custom (Mil-Spec)

  • Exact length for your setup
  • Multiple width options
  • Your choice of end configurations
  • Professional-grade materials
  • Often cheaper in the long run (no exchanges)

✗ Off-the-Shelf

  • Limited length increments (usually 2" steps)
  • Fixed width and hardware
  • May require adapters or drilling
  • Quality varies wildly by brand

Red Flags — What to Watch For

  • No material spec listed — If a seller can't tell you what their webbing is made of, don't buy it
  • No tensile strength rating — Legitimate manufacturers always list breaking strength
  • Price too good to be true — A 2-pack of 20" limit straps for $15 is using the cheapest possible materials
  • No stitching detail — Look for bar-tack or box-X stitching at load points
  • Imported but marketed as premium — look for MIL-W-4088 spec and USA manufacturing. Most cheap straps on Amazon are neither.

Chains vs. Nylon Webbing Limit Straps

A lot of racers — especially in flat dirt track, short course, and circle track — still use chains to limit suspension travel. It works, but it's a rough solution. When a chain hits full extension, it stops hard. There's zero give. That shock loads straight into your mounting points, axle housing, and frame — and over time it cracks mounts, bends tabs, and beats up components that are expensive to replace.

Nylon webbing limit straps do the same job but absorb energy on extension instead of slamming to a dead stop. The webbing has natural stretch that cushions the end of travel, which means:

  • Less stress on mounts and frame — no metal-on-metal shock loading
  • Smoother ride at full droop — the strap decelerates gradually instead of a hard stop
  • Lighter weight — nylon webbing weighs a fraction of chain
  • No rattle — chains clank and rattle at speed; straps are silent
  • Easier to size — cut to exact length vs. counting chain links

If you're still running chains as limit straps, switching to proper nylon webbing straps is one of the cheapest upgrades you can make — and your suspension mounts will thank you.

What Are Suspension Limit Straps?

Suspension limit straps — also called droop straps or travel limiters — prevent a vehicle's suspension from dropping below a safe limit when a wheel goes into full droop (hangs in the air). Without limit straps, full droop can cause CV axles to over-extend and tear, brake lines to stretch and crack, and ABS sensor wiring to snap. Limit straps are a cheap insurance policy against expensive drivetrain damage on lifted vehicles used off-road.

They are especially important after a suspension lift. A 4-inch lift adds 4 inches of downward travel range that the factory axles, lines, and wiring were not designed to handle. Properly set limit straps restrict that travel to a safe range — typically allowing 2–3 inches of additional droop over stock while protecting the drivetrain components.

How to Measure and Set Limit Straps

Measurement process: with the vehicle on a lift or jackstands with the wheels hanging, measure the distance from the upper mounting point to the lower mounting point at the maximum safe droop position. This is determined by checking CV axle angle (should not exceed 30–35 degrees), brake line tension, and ABS sensor wire slack. Set the strap length to this measurement — the strap should become taut just before the problematic droop point.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need limit straps with a suspension lift?

Yes, for any lift over 2 inches used off-road. Full droop on a lifted vehicle without limit straps can tear CV axles, snap brake lines, and damage ABS wiring. Limit straps are inexpensive insurance against expensive drivetrain repairs.

What is the difference between limit straps and bump stops?

Bump stops limit upward suspension travel (compression). Limit straps limit downward travel (droop/extension). Both are needed for a complete suspension protection setup. Many off-road builds run extended bump stops and limit straps together.

How long do limit straps last?

Quality nylon limit straps last 5–10 years under normal off-road use. Inspect them annually for fraying, UV degradation, and cracking at the mounting hardware. Replace immediately if you see fraying — a snapped limit strap at full droop can damage the components it was protecting.
